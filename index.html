Abstract

Abstract

Striking a balance between helpfulness and safety remains a fundamental challenge in aligning large language models. To achieve this balance, models should refuse harmful queries (e.g., “How do I shoot someone?”) while remaining responsive to benign inputs, even those superficially resembling harmful queries (e.g., “Where can I shoot a good photo?”). However, models often struggle to distinguish genuinely harmful queries from benign queries that contain superficially risky language, resulting in false refusals. In this paper, we address the issue by decomposing a response in the safety-tuning dataset into two distinct components: (i) a boilerplate refusal statement and (ii) a rationale explaining the refusal. Our experiments and analyses show that refusal statements impede accurate discrimination between harmful and benign queries by inducing reliance on superficial cues. In contrast, training solely on rationales reduces false refusals while maintaining a comparable level of safety performance. Rationale-Only benefits also appear in our ICL configuration and remain compatible with the evaluated inference-time mitigation methods. The results emphasize the necessity of precisely curated, fine-grained safety supervision datasets and outline directions for constructing aligned agents that better reconcile helpfulness with safety.

02 · Method

Safety response decomposition

We categorize the elements of a safety-tuning response into two primary components: a boilerplate refusal statement and a rationale explaining the refusal. Responses are first normalized to a base format, and variants are constructed by holding the request and rationale content fixed while varying the target-response structure.

Refusal statementI’m sorry, but I cannot help you with that. Refusal rationaleCounterfeiting money is illegal and …

Refusal statement. A short, often formulaic expression of non-compliance used for potentially harmful requests, e.g., “Sorry, but I can’t help with that request” and “I’m unable to assist with that request”.

Refusal rationale. Explains why the request is refused by identifying request-specific concerns such as illegality, physical harm, or ethical risk.

Position and components of the refusal statement

Placing the refusal statement at the beginning yields more false refusals than placing it in the middle or at the end. The inclusion of a refusal statement elevates false refusal rates, regardless of whether it is presented alone or alongside a rationale. Conversely, Rationale-Only models exhibit reduced false refusals while maintaining comparable safety performance.

Explicitly stating prompt details in rationales influences distinction capability

Request-Specific rationales provide an additional benefit in reducing false refusals compared to Generic alternatives. This pattern suggests that naming the requested action and its risk provides a more informative supervision signal than a generic explanation.

04 · Analysis

Internal and structural effects of refusal statements

We evaluate internal effects via decoding entropy and token-level attribution, then test neutral prefixes, varied phrasing, and keyword-specific placements of refusal statements. Results are reported for Llama-3.1-8B.

Boilerplate statements and their association with deterministic behavior and superficial cues

Statement and Rationale yields lower first-token entropy than Rationale-Only on harmful and pseudo-harmful prompts, indicating a more concentrated initial-token distribution. Removing isolated risky tokens produces larger entropy shifts under Statement and Rationale, whereas Rationale-Only sensitivity is distributed over more semantically informative spans.

Manual inspection of attribution types

Manual inspection indicates that while Rationale-Only training produces meaningful attributions in over 97% of cases, Statement and Rationale training frequently relies on less content-related cues in approximately 90% of instances.

Table 5 of the paper; XSTest-Safe n = 79, OKTest n = 74.

Structural and stylistic variations

Models trained with a neutral prefix (“Thank you for asking!”) continue to exhibit higher false refusal rates than Rationale-Only, despite the prefix containing no explicit safety-related language. Replacing the statement with 15 manually authored stylistic variants leads to observable improvements relative to the fixed statement condition, but performance remains consistently below that of Rationale-Only models.
